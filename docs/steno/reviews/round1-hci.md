# Steno mode, round 1: HCI review

Reviewer stance: mobile text entry and gesture interfaces (marking menus, novice-to-expert transition, Fitts and steering modelling), refereeing as I would for CHI or UIST. I read `DESIGN.md`, the three research reports, `src/model.js`, `src/recognizer.js`, `src/robot.js`, `src/ui/guide.js`, `src/ui/keyboard.js`, `src/engine.js`, `src/planner.js`, `src/packs.js` and all four tools. I re-ran every tool in a scratch copy, reproduced the generated theory byte for byte (same md5, `dc42bfd9…`), and then wrote my own Monte Carlo, adversarial and sensitivity scripts and drove the live demo with real pointer events. The repository was not modified apart from this file.

Reproduction scripts and outputs are in the session scratch directory (`scratch/review/hci/`): `aspect.mjs`, `diag.mjs` and `mc.mjs` (recogniser), `eval2.mjs` (cost-model sensitivity), `shell.mjs` and `letters_rt.mjs` (what the translator types), and `../../pw/hci_live.mjs` (live demo). The screenshots are under `shots/` in that directory. Because the scratch directory may be cleared, every finding below also states its numbers and enough of the method to redo it.

## Scores

The owner's bar is 9.2. This submission is a long way from it. My reason is not that the idea is bad. The engine is careful work, the corpus pipeline is reproducible and its hold-out survives a stricter test than the one the designer ran, and the 48 word flicks are a real, modest win. But the recogniser fails on the prototype's own key geometry, the evaluation leaves out the costs that decide whether gesture shortcuts pay off, the 2,624 prefix briefs turn typos and ordinary domain words into other words, the shell use case is broken by the English dictionary underneath it, and most of the 178 hooks are arbitrary pairings that add almost no speed once recall and errors are counted. At a top venue I would score this a reject with encouragement to resubmit after a motor study.

## What the design gets right

These should survive any revision. Steno mode is a superset of the letter keyboard, so there is no "binary drop" for out-of-vocabulary words, which is the single most important lesson from both the stenography and the gesture-keyboard literature. The translator is deterministic and context-free, keeps one undo per stroke, and owns spacing and capitals, which is a faithful port of Plover's model. The theory is generated by program from a stated cost model, and I could regenerate it exactly. The held-out split is sound: I re-split the film corpus by whole movie rather than by interleaved utterance (`movie_id % 10 == 7`) and the full-theory saving moved from 47.6 % to 47.4 %, so topic leakage does not flatter the numbers. Endings that replace the space rather than add a stroke are a nice idea. The guide's second level, which shows each curl where the finger would have to go once a direction is chosen, is a sensible OctoPocus-style continuation display. And `DESIGN.md` is unusually candid in its caveats.

## Findings, most severe first

### 1. On the prototype's 105 × 66 pt keys the recogniser fails for horizontal motion, including on the letter page that is claimed to be unchanged

`preprocess` divides every x coordinate by the key's aspect ratio (`src/recognizer.js:29`). On the current keyboard the keys are square, so this does nothing. The prototype's three-column grid makes the letter keys 105 × 66 pt (`src/ui/keyboard.js:12`), an aspect of 1.59, so every horizontal millimetre now counts as 0.63 of a millimetre. The consequences, measured with `aspect.mjs` against `classify` and `classifyStroke`:

- The shortest straight horizontal flick that is not a tap is 38 pt (6.1 mm) at a deliberate speed and 24 pt (3.8 mm) when fast, against 22 pt and 13.5 pt on the current square key. Vertical flicks are unaffected.
- The eight direction sectors are no longer 45° each on the glass. East and west are 29° wide, north and south 67°, and the diagonals 42°. A thumb that aims horizontally now has ±14.6° of tolerance instead of ±22.5°.
- For a median 4.5 mm flick with 8° of angular scatter, east and west flicks fail 34 % of the time on the 105 × 66 key (28 % read as a tap, 6.5 % as the wrong direction), against about 1 % on a square key. The most frequent word flicks sit exactly there: *you* (T-e), *that* (T-w), *with* (E-w), *for* (S-e), *are* (A-w), *like* (N-w), *it* (I-e), *he* (H-w), *my* (R-e).
- Hooks drawn with a physical 90° corner and legs of 3.8 and 3.5 mm are recognised correctly only 18 to 62 % of the time depending on the first direction (east 20 %, west 18 %); the identical strokes on a square key score 99 to 100 %. Legs of about 5.8 mm are needed to reach 95 % on the wide key (`diag.mjs`).

I confirmed this in the live demo with real pointer events (`hci_live.mjs`, results in `shots/live-report.txt`): a 32 pt (5.1 mm) flick east on T types the letter "T", and only a 40 pt flick types "You". On the **letter page**, a 26 pt flick east on O types "o" instead of "b". A hook drawn up 24 pt and right 22 pt on T, meant as *there*, types "Y". A hook drawn right 24 pt and down 22 pt on T, meant as *you're*, types "Your", which is the textbook real-word error.

The robustness test cannot see any of this, because the synthetic finger multiplies its x coordinates by the same aspect before drawing (`src/robot.js:37`, `:58`), so it draws in the classifier's own normalised space; because it uses a 98.67 × 68 key rather than the demo's 105 × 66 (`tools/robustness.mjs:14`); and because it tests only key O. The design document's statements that the letter page is "unchanged" (`DESIGN.md:13`) and that the turn sectors are "scale-free" (`DESIGN.md:78`) are therefore both false for this prototype. The turn angle is computed in stretched space, and the length gates (`HOOK.minFirst`, `HOOK.minSecond`, `recognizer.js:173-174`) are not scale-free at all.

### 2. Word flicks next to word flicks produce real-word substitutions, and the assignment objective never prices them

Even with the aspect bug fixed (square keys, `mc.mjs` T1 with `KW=KH=66`), the frequency-weighted share of word flicks read as a *different word* is about 1 % at 9° of angular scatter and 5 % at 12°. The top substitutions are *you → your*, *the → think*, *to → your*, *to → this*, *in → if*, *it → I'm*, *is → I'm*. On the prototype's wide keys the share rises to 2.8 to 4.4 % at 9° and 6.7 to 9.2 % at 12°, with *you → your*, *it → I'm*, *it → if*, *my → really*, *for → should*, *for → sorry*, *that → think* and *that → this* on top.

This is worse than it looks, for two reasons. A misdirected flick on the old keyboard produced a stray symbol that a reader sees at once; here it produces a grammatical-looking wrong word, the error class that proofreading is worst at catching. And the layout puts grammatical substitutes side by side: *he / him / her* are H-w, H-nw and H-sw; *this / to / that* are T-sw, T-s and T-w; *it / if / I'm* are I-e, I-ne and I-se; *you / your* are T-e and T-se. The within-key placement in `tools/theory.mjs:89-98` rewards pointing at the second letter's key and being on an axis, and has no term for confusability. The research report asked for the clarity metric of Smith, Bi and Zhai to be reported (§5, §6.3). It was not computed.

The space-bar endings have the same problem. With 12° of scatter on 30 pt flicks, 88.6 % of ending flicks are read correctly; the errors are *-ing → -s*, *-ed → -s*, *-er → -ly*, *'s → -ly*, each about 7 % of that ending, and 6 to 8 % of each ending latch into a caret slide instead, because `slideSpace` decides on the first sample that is 12 pt out and within 25° of level (`src/ui/keyboard.js:274-284`). A caret that silently moves one character means the next word is typed in the wrong place.

### 3. Prefix briefs rewrite typos, names and ordinary domain words into other words

Phase 4 gives 2,624 words a spelled prefix (`tools/theory.mjs:246-261`), down to 3 per million, and a prefix only has to be absent from the chat lexicon (`theory.mjs:244`). The consequences, measured against the generated theory:

- **Typos become different words.** Among words of five or more letters in the top 5,000, dropping the last letter yields a brief for a *different* word in 15.3 % of tokens, and dropping one interior letter does so in 7.2 %. Examples: *woul → woulda*, *coul → coulda*, *sorr → sorrow*, *afte → afterward*, *othe → otherwise*, *grea → greatest*, *whic → whichever*, *hous → houston*, *happ → happen* (meant *happy*), *thes → thesis*, *thre → threat*, *migh → mighty*. In the held-out SMS corpus typed letter by letter, *whic*, *afte*, *onl* and *fai* came out as *whichever*, *afterward*, *online* and *faith* (`letters_rt.mjs`).
- **Completions are not monotone.** *cou* is *could*, but *coul* is *coulda*. "Once enough of it has been spelled to be unambiguous" is not what the generator does: it assigns the shortest unclaimed prefix to each word in frequency order, so a longer prefix can belong to a different word.
- **Wrong grey completions appear while spelling ordinary words.** For words with no stroke of their own, a completion for a *different* word appears mid-word in 14.5 % of tokens: *great* shows *greatest* after *grea*, *stay* shows *stand*, *stop* shows *story*, *play* shows *place*, *these* shows *thesis*. This competes for the glance budget that Jiang et al. (2020) show is already 60 to 70 % on the keyboard.
- **Real words outside the chat lexicon are rewritten.** 244 of the top 20,000 web words (and 838 of the top 50,000) are brief outlines: *prev → previous*, *var → various*, *const → constant*, *dir → direct*, *opt → option*, *proc → process*, *alt → although*, *mem → memory*, *eval → evaluation*, *obj → object*, *uri → urine*, *literal → literally*, *gran → grandma*, *pun → punch*. The name *Bea* becomes *Beautiful*.
- **They cannot be learned.** The median brief word occurs 8.3 times per million. At 1,000 words a day, M3's payback of about 18.5 expert uses would take 318 weeks. So the brief row of the evaluation is not a learning stage; it is deterministic word completion, whose monitoring cost the model sets to zero (finding 5).

### 4. The shell use case is broken, because the English theory stays live underneath the shell pack

Packs only shadow entries; they cannot mask the dictionary below them (`src/packs.js:111-123`). So in the terminal context every English brief still fires on a space. Typed letter by letter with space-bar taps (`shell.mjs`), with the shell pack on exactly as `setContext('terminal')` configures it:

| Typed | Comes out as |
| --- | --- |
| `cat notes.py` | `cat notes.python3` |
| `python3 main.py` | `python3 main.python3` |
| `awk` | `awkward` |
| `uniq -c` | `unique -c` |
| `env` | `environment` |
| `cd dist` | `cd distance` |
| `npm run init` | `npm run initial` |
| `make prod` | `make product` |
| `const` | `constant` |

The shell pack itself also shadows real commands (`nm` becomes `--name`, `gs`, `tf` and `py` are taken), and steno mode removes `$ : ; @ * ( ) [ ] { } _ \ #` from the keys, so variables, globs, `user@host:path`, snake_case names and subshells all need a page switch. For "writing for CLIs with flags", which the owner named explicitly, this is not yet usable.

### 5. The cost model is internally inconsistent and omits the costs that decide the outcome

A word flick costs 300 ms and a letter 262 ms, but a hook costs 262 ms plus two Cao and Zhai line segments of 202 and 238 ms (`src/model.js:15-18`). A hook's first segment is exactly a flick's movement, yet it is costed at 202 ms in the hook and about 38 ms in the flick. Either flicks are underpriced or hooks overpriced. Beyond that, the base model has no recall time, no error rate, no cost of noticing and undoing an error, and no cost for watching the composer to learn when a brief has completed. The only sensitivity analysis is three variants (`tools/evaluate.mjs:269`).

I re-ran the evaluation with the assumptions made explicit (`eval2.mjs`; same corpus, planner and stages). The left number is dialogue (DailyDialog), the right is SMS, both as gain over the letter keyboard.

| Scenario | 48 flicks + endings | + all 178 hooks | + all briefs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| As published | +34 % / +14 % | +55 % / +27 % | +74 % / +35 % |
| Flick costed consistently with the hook (464 ms) | +27 % / +11 % | +46 % / +23 % | +62 % / +30 % |
| Plus recall of 300 ms per flick or ending and 500 ms per hook | +14 % / +4 % | +18 % / +7 % | +28 % / +12 % |
| Plus 4 % flick, 8 % hook and 6 % ending errors, each costing 600 ms to notice, an undo and a redo | +12 % / +3 % | +13 % / +4 % | +22 % / +9 % |
| Plus 500 ms to watch for a brief's completion | +12 % / +3 % | +13 % / +4 % | +15 % / +4 % |
| An average one-thumb typist (letter 400 ms, about 30 WPM), last-but-one assumptions | +16 % / +7 % | +21 % / +11 % | +32 % / +17 % |

Two conclusions follow, and neither is in the design document. First, once recall and errors are counted, the 178 hooks add about one percentage point on top of the flicks and endings. They are the bulk of what a learner is asked to memorise, and they buy almost nothing. Second, the robust gain is the 48 flicks plus endings, worth roughly 10 to 15 % on dialogue and 3 to 7 % on real texts. That is a respectable result for 54 items, and it is the result the paper should lead with. The headline "79.8 WPM" in `DESIGN.md:146` should go: it is about 1.5 times what real one-thumb typists reach (30 WPM, Palin et al. 2019) and above the roughly 67 WPM at which composition, rather than the method, becomes the limit (Kristensson and Vertanen 2014). Both facts are in the designer's own research report.

### 6. "A person who learns nothing loses nothing" is contradicted by the designer's own evaluator

The table in `DESIGN.md:136-146` shows only DailyDialog, where letters-only steno mode is 1 % faster than the letter keyboard (from free spaces after marks). `tools/evaluate.mjs` also prints the SMS corpus, where letters-only steno mode is *slower*, 44.6 against 45.8 WPM. Part of that is a corpus artifact (221 SMS messages contain the NUS anonymisation token `<#>`), but after removing it, 12.3 % of real SMS messages still need a symbol that steno mode took off the keys, mostly `:` and `)` for times and smileys. `DESIGN.md:18` calls these symbols that a text message "almost never needs". Beyond speed, a letters-only user also loses on errors: finding 1 (horizontal flicks on the wide keys), and the return swipe. Of the return swipes that the existing classifier accepts as capitals (`ratio < 0.5`), 11 to 18 % are read by `classifyStroke` as hooks, concentrated at turns of 120 to 140°, so a slightly sloppy capital types a word (`mc.mjs` T3; live demo: a return swipe on O at a 120° turn types "Out" instead of "U"). Finally, on a fresh install the guide flashes its whole map on every letter tap (finding 7).

### 7. The guide's "fades with use" policy contradicts the evidence it cites, and never fades for letter typists

`mapDelay` returns `min(350, practice × 25)` ms (`src/ui/guide.js:29`), where `practice` is the number of word strokes made from that key (`src/ui/keyboard.js:404`, `:428`). This has three problems.

- Letters never count, so a person who types only letters sees the full map unfold at 0 ms on every touch, forever. `shots/dark-tap-E-70ms.png` shows the full map 70 ms into an ordinary tap on E on a fresh install.
- While practice is low, the guide is always on, which is exactly the traceable, always-present guide that Anderson and Bischof (2013) found best in training and worst at retention. The research report's own rules say to show the guide only after a hesitation of 250 to 500 ms (rule 2) and not to keep a tracing template on screen (rule 4).
- Practice is counted per key, not per stroke. Fourteen uses of *the* make every other T stroke, including hooks never used, wait the full 350 ms. After about 126 word strokes on the nine keys the adaptive policy is identical to the keyboard's old fixed delay, so in practice "fades with use" means "flashes for the first few minutes".

### 8. Guide placement and stability

The map rides above the key, which is the right call for thumb occlusion: in every screenshot the card sits above the touched key, away from where the body of a thumb lies. But for the top row it covers the composer and the send button, which is where the word being spelled and its grey completion are shown (`shots/dark-I-hook-ccw.png`, `shots/dark-A-hook-cw.png`, `shots/mobile-H-hook-ccw.png`). For the left column the card is clamped to x = −14 (`guide.js:69`), so the counter-clockwise chip is cut off at the screen edge (*time* on T and *any* on A in `shots/dark-T-hook-cw.png`, `shots/mobile-T-hook-cw.png`, `shots/dark-A-hook-cw.png`). Chips also overlap the lit cell and each other (*family* over *for* in `shots/mobile-S-hook-back.png`). And mid-hook the lit direction is computed from the far point of the path, which drifts as the second leg develops: a stroke begun south-west on I is displayed as *is* (south) with *is*'s curls while the finger turns (`shots/dark-I-hook-ccw.png`), and then reverts to the south-west flick on release. Feedforward that changes its mind mid-stroke undermines the rehearsal argument.

### 9. The hooks are mostly not "relatives", and the contraction rule holds for under half of the contractions

`DESIGN.md:20-26` says a curl types a word related to the flicked one. By token mass, 9 % of hook use is contractions, 2 % kin and 23 % words that share at least two letters with their flick. The remaining 66 % share only a first letter: *the → time*, *with → was / why / work*, *what → well*, *so → sure / still*, *to → take / tell*, *me → maybe*, *my → meet / might / many*, *I'm → inside*, *it → important / information*. These are arbitrary codes, which the research report's rule 9 warns against. The contraction rule ("right for is/are/am, left for not/will, back for have", applied to "every word that has them") holds for 17 contractions. Six are on the wrong turn or not on a hook (*didn't* on clockwise, *she's* on counter-clockwise, *there's* and *let's* on back, *I'm* and *it's* as flicks), and fifteen common ones have no stroke at all, including *can't*, *they're*, *doesn't*, *wasn't*, *couldn't*, *we'll*, *they'll*, *wouldn't* and *shouldn't*. Meanwhile very common words such as *get*, *got*, *one*, *did*, *our*, *who* and *been* have no stroke, because all of O's nine letters (b c d g j o p q u) compete for its sixteen hook slots and it has no free flick directions.

### 10. The round-trip test is a self-consistency check, not evidence about users

"2,437 held-out messages … All of them pass" (`DESIGN.md:164`) is true, but the test draws only on DailyDialog and the film corpus, never SMS (`test/roundtrip.test.mjs:42`); turns the texting pack off (`:14`); skips 563 messages for characters or casing (`:48`); and generates each script from the same dictionary it then checks. `scriptFor` returns null, and the test skips the message, whenever a spelled word collides with an abbreviation (`src/script.js:53`), which is exactly the failure class in findings 3 and 4. It proves that the planner and translator agree. It does not show that what a person types comes out.

### 11. Smaller defects

- The texting pack rewrites real words: *atm* becomes *at the moment* (the cash machine), *tn* becomes *tonight*, *def* becomes *definitely* (`src/packs.js:68-76`).
- Endings are applied without checking that the result is a word: *and* with the up-right flick gives *anding* (seen in the live demo).
- The robustness test passes `peakTime = t / 2000` (`tools/robustness.mjs:31`), half the real value in seconds, which makes the "fast flick" rule fire more readily than it would on a device.
- Its accuracy falls from 97.6 % at sloppiness 1 to 81.7 % at 1.5, with hooks at 77 %. The margin is thin, and "sloppiness 1" is not calibrated to any thumb data.
- In steno mode there is no character-level delete once a word was written by a stroke: turning *your* into *you* is an undo plus a re-stroke. That is faithful to stenography but should be stated.
- Zero on the number page is a flick down from 8 or 5, which the document flags itself.
- Brief words include film-corpus junk and names (*woulda*, *watchin*, *drinkin*, *houston*, *jason*, *nicole*), because the floor is 3 per million.

## Novelty

The design document is honest that each primitive is old, and it names the combination as the contribution. After checking, I think the combination is narrower than claimed.

KeyScretch (Costagliola, Fuccella and colleagues, IUI 2011; IEEE THMS 2014) already lets a stroke that starts on a soft-keyboard key, guided by a radial menu around that key, enter a multi-letter chunk, with compound strokes for longer chunks and model-predicted gains of 30 to 49 % over tapping. That is the core of "a flick is a word, a curl is its relative", on QWERTY rather than a 3 × 3 grid. Prefix completion that fires on a delimiter is ordinary deterministic abbreviation expansion (iOS Text Replacement, Android shortcuts, Shieber and Nelken 2007, and stenography's own briefs). Inflection keys that apply *-s*, *-ing* and *-ed* to the previous word with irregular tables are long-standing in AAC (Minspeak morphology icons, Proloquo2Go's grammar forms, Compansion). Per-item fading of guidance is in Anderson and Bischof (2013), ExposeHK and Skillometers (Malacria et al. 2013). Phrase-to-gesture keyboards ship commercially (Gesturely, 2015). I found no evidence that MessagEase or Thumb-Key ship word-level flicks, so that check comes out in the designer's favour.

What remains new, as far as I can tell, is: a corpus-optimised, deterministic word layer placed in the symbol directions of a MessagEase letter grid, so that the word mode is a superset of the letter mode; and endings on the space bar that replace the space. That is a reasonable design increment, not a "truly novel" contribution, and a reviewer would expect it to be backed by a measured motor and learning study, which is where the genuine contribution would be (the research report says the same in §6.6).

## Learnability

The learner is asked to memorise 232 strokes (48 flicks, 178 hooks, 6 endings), plus 2,624 brief boundaries that in practice can only be found by watching the composer. The support is four rules, six one-sentence lessons, a coach chip that shows a shorter stroke once after a word is spelled, and the guide. There is no curriculum, no spaced introduction, no record of which strokes the person knows, and no recall check.

The first hour will go well: the person types letters as before, notices that *the*, *you*, *to* and *I'm* are one flick each, and adopts perhaps ten to twenty flicks, because those words occur dozens of times an hour. The first week is where it stalls. The median hook word occurs about four times a week at 1,000 words a day, so reaching the 6 to 10 exposures before first recall that Zheng et al. (2018) measured takes two weeks per hook, and two-thirds of hook mass has no mnemonic beyond its first letter. Nothing stops a person from typing letters forever except the coach chip, and Gutwin, Cockburn and Lafreniere (2015) show that rehearsal alone does not produce switching. The design has no mechanism for the intermodal switch beyond exposure.

## Design-level objections that no bug fix cures

1. **Function words in adjacent 45° sectors.** As long as *you / your*, *he / him / her*, *this / that / to* and *it / if / I'm* sit next to each other, angular scatter turns into real-word substitutions. I would add a confusion term to the assignment objective (frequency × probability of an adjacent misread × how hard the substitution is to notice), separate grammatical substitutes, and report the clarity metric.
2. **Arbitrary hooks.** A hundred and seventy-eight pairings, two-thirds without a mnemonic, used a few times a week each, worth about one percentage point. I would keep only rule-governed families: a complete contraction system on consistent turns covering all of the roughly 38 common contractions, and perhaps inflections. The other slots should be left for the user's own strokes.
3. **Prefix briefs on plain space.** They cannot be learned, they need monitoring, and they turn typos and domain words into other words. I would remove them from the default theory, or require an explicit accept gesture (a space-bar flick, say) rather than firing on a plain space, and never fire them underneath a non-prose pack.
4. **Changing the fallback.** The three-column relayout changes the letter page's geometry and so its accuracy. Either keep the existing key shapes or classify in physical millimetres with isotropic sectors, and then measure.
5. **Learning left to the user.** A word layer that people will not adopt has no value. It needs a curriculum driven by the person's own typing log: introduce 10 to 15 strokes per session in order of personal benefit, fade guidance per stroke, and show progress.

## The study needed to believe the headline numbers

Two studies, in this order.

**A motor study** with 16 to 24 participants, one-thumb and two-thumb, on the current square keys and the proposed wide keys, eliciting letter flicks, word flicks, all three hook turns, return swipes, endings and caret slides, both with targets shown and from memory. It should log full kinematics so that the synthetic finger can be calibrated against real strokes, and it should report per-class confusion matrices, time per stroke class, and the share of errors that produce a valid wrong word. My prediction with the current recogniser: 15 to 35 % failures on horizontal flicks and hooks on the wide keys. With the geometry fixed: hook errors of 5 to 10 % (M3's 2.9 % was on tiles twice this size), and word-flick substitutions of 2 to 5 %.

**A longitudinal learning and field study** with 12 to 16 participants: ten sessions of 20 to 30 minutes over two to three weeks, then two weeks in the wild with logging, comparing letter mode with steno mode and, within steno mode, the adaptive guide against a fixed 350 ms guide. It should measure entry rate on both transcription and composition, corrected and uncorrected error rates (including real-word substitutions left in sent text), stroke share per class, recall with the guide off at 24 hours and at one week, and NASA-TLX. My prediction: after ten sessions people use 25 to 40 of the 48 flicks and fewer than 30 hooks; speed rises 5 to 15 % over letter mode on dialogue and 0 to 5 % on their own messages, with a higher uncorrected error rate; in the wild, word flicks plateau at 15 to 25 % of words, hooks at under 5 %, and briefs are used only as autocomplete. Nobody reaches the modelled 60 to 80 WPM. Typical one-thumb participants would go from about 25 to 35 WPM in letter mode to about 30 to 40.

## Changes that would most raise my score, in priority order

1. Fix the recognition geometry: classify in physical units with isotropic sectors (or keep square keys), then rebuild the robustness test with the real key size, all nine keys, strokes drawn on the glass rather than in normalised space, rounded corners, release tails and sloppy return swipes. Publish the confusion matrices.
2. Add a confusion cost to the flick and ending assignment, report the clarity metric, and move grammatical substitutes apart.
3. Take prefix briefs out of the default, or make them accept-on-gesture. Let a pack mask the dictionaries below it, so the shell pack runs without the English briefs, and remove the colliding texting entries.
4. Redesign the fade: per-stroke expertise, a minimum hesitation of at least 250 ms after a short introduction, no map on letter taps, letters counting toward familiarity, the top-row guide kept off the composer, and chips clamped on screen.
5. Redo the evaluation: consistent stroke costs, recall growing with vocabulary size, error and correction costs, a monitoring cost for completions, all three corpora in the document's table (including the SMS letters-only regression), and gains stated as relative rather than as absolute WPM.
6. Cut the hooks to rule-governed families, complete the contraction system, and hand the other slots to the user.
7. Build the learning loop: a curriculum from the person's own log, 10 to 15 new strokes per session, spaced exposure, recall checks, and a visible adoption measure.
8. Run the motor study before any further design iteration, and narrow the novelty claim to what survives KeyScretch.
