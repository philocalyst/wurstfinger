# Steno mode, round 2: HCI review

Reviewer stance: mobile text entry and gesture interfaces (marking menus, gesture keyboards, novice-to-expert transition, Fitts and steering modelling), refereeing as I would for CHI or UIST. I read `DESIGN.md`, the first round of reviews, the three research reports, `docs/layout-science.md`, and in the prototype `src/model.js`, `src/geometry.js`, `src/layout.js`, `src/recognizer.js`, `src/robot.js`, `src/engine.js`, `src/packs.js`, `src/planner.js`, `src/script.js`, `src/ui/guide.js`, `src/ui/keyboard.js`, `src/ui/app.js`, `src/ui/panel.js`, all five tools and the three test files. I re-ran the tools in a scratch copy, reproduced `data/build/*.json` and `src/theory.generated.js` byte for byte (theory md5 `1940d3ae…`), varied the assumptions in copies, wrote my own Monte Carlo and adversarial tests against `classify`, `classifyStroke` and `classifySpace` on the real 94 × 70 pt key, drove the live demo with real mouse events, and took mid-stroke screenshots on every row and column in both themes on desktop and mobile. The repository was not modified apart from this file.

Scripts and outputs are in the session scratch directory, `scratch/review2/hci/`: `mc.mjs` (thumb Monte Carlo), `adv.mjs` (adversarial strokes), `robust_split.mjs` (the designer's own synthetic thumb with misreads split by direction), `flicker.mjs` (live preview stability), `eval2.mjs` (cost-model sensitivity), `theorystats.mjs` (theory composition), `live_out.txt` (live pointer results, produced by `../../pw/r2hci_live.mjs`), and `shots/` (screenshots, produced by `../../pw/r2hci_shots.mjs`). The owner's bar is 9.2. This iteration is clearly better than the first: the geometry bug is gone, the prefix briefs no longer fire on a space, the terminal is exact, the guide follows the hesitation rule, and the document is more candid. I would now call it a promising design with a real, modest core (the 48 word flicks and the space-bar endings). It is not yet something I would accept at a top venue or recommend people use as their default keyboard, for three reasons that a reader of `DESIGN.md` would not discover. The recogniser's fallback for a failed curl is not what the design says it is, and the one-move recovery built on that claim fixes only a small minority of errors. The classifier has no defence against the small tail a thumb leaves when it lifts, and every flick on the keyboard, letters included, now has words on its curls, so that tail types a word. And the corner key puts "send" between the two page-switch flicks, so a 23 degree aiming error sends the message, or in the terminal runs the half-typed command.

**How I weigh the missing user study.** The brief was a design and a working demo to try, not a paper, so I do not mark the work down simply because nobody has measured it. I do mark down claims that go further than the evidence, and calibration that was cheap and was not done (the demo already runs on a phone with `HOST=0.0.0.0`, so a few hundred logged thumb strokes are an afternoon's work). Of the gap between 5.4 and 9.2, I attribute roughly 1 to 1.5 points to what only measurement can settle: the time and error rate of a thumb curl on a 10.9 mm key decide whether the 188 hooks are worth learning, and no amount of modelling can answer that. The remaining 2.5 points or so are things the designer can fix now.

## What the second iteration got right

These deserve to be said first, because several are now solid. Classification is in physical units with isotropic sectors, and in the live demo every one of the 63 word flicks on the eight word keys, drawn at 4.5 mm, typed exactly what its label says. The terminal stack contains no English, and `cat notes.py`, `uniq -c`, `cd dist`, `nm -D lib.so`, `echo $HOME`, `ls *.md | wc -l` and `ssh me@host:~/x` all came out exactly; `g`, `c` and a pull on the space bar gave `git commit -m ""` with the caret between the quotes. Completions fire only on an explicit pull of the space bar, and none of the twelve prefixes that produced wrong grey offers in round one (`grea`, `stay`, `stop`, `thes`, `coul`, `woul`, `happ`, …) offers anything now. The guide appears on a 300 ms rest rather than on contact, shows nothing on a tap, sits beside the key for the top row so the composer is never covered, and with clean strokes the preview never changes its mind mid-hook (0 of 188 hooks, `flicker.mjs`). The corner key now has a held drag for letter-by-letter deletion. There is a nice piece of cross-key consistency that I did not credit enough last time: right means "is" and left means "not" both on a curl and on the space bar (`'s` below right, `n't` below left), so *we* plus the lower-right ending is *we're* and *was* plus lower-left is *wasn't*. The held-out split survives a stricter test again: splitting DailyDialog by whole dialogue rather than by utterance left every row of the evaluation unchanged to within 0.05 strokes per word (47 % fewer strokes either way).

## Current findings, most severe first

### 1. A failed curl becomes the neighbouring diagonal's word, not its own flick, so "change" rarely fixes it, and the document says the opposite

`DESIGN.md` (lines 189 to 191) says that at 3.5 mm legs "nearly all of the loss is curls read as their own plain flick", and the Numbers panel tells the user that a misread curl "nearly always comes out as its own plain flick … which the corner key's 'change' puts right in one move". That is not what the recogniser does. When a corner is found but a hook gate fails, `classifyStroke` returns `base.dir`, the robust direction toward the farthest point of the path, and for an L-shaped path that is the diagonal between the two legs. `tools/robustness.mjs` cannot see this because it counts "cw → flick" without checking the flick's direction.

With the designer's own synthetic thumb and grid parameters (`robust_split.mjs`, key O, 3,200 curls per cell), misread curls land on their own flick 0.1 % of the time and on another flick 2.1 % of the time at 4.5 mm legs and 9 degrees of aim error; at 3.5 mm the figures are 0.4 % and 12.2 %. In my own Monte Carlo (finding 3), only 15 % of all wrong outputs from word strokes are on the same key and first direction, which is the only family "change" steps through. Live, a curl drawn as one smooth arc starting north on T typed "Y" (the north-east letter flick), and the same on E starting west typed "What" (`live_out.txt`). This is the central recovery story of the design, and it is currently false.

### 2. A lift-off tail turns a flick into a word on its curl, on word flicks and letter flicks alike

Thumbs leave a small displacement as they lift. The old classifier ignored it, because only the far point mattered. `classifyStroke` accepts a second leg of 12 pt (1.9 mm) at more than 45 degrees, and every flick on the steno page now has words on its turns, because hooks were given to all the letter flicks too. In `adv.mjs`, a clean 4.5 mm flick followed by a 2.5 mm tail at 60 to 120 degrees turns *the* into *there* and *you* into *you're*; a 2 mm tail at 150 degrees turns them into *then* and *you've*. Both reproduced live with real pointer events ("There", "You're"). The synthetic thumb never produces a tail, so the robustness grid is blind to this. How large the effect is depends on a quantity nobody has measured; as a sensitivity, if 15 % of strokes carry a tail with a 1.2 mm median, 10.6 % of word flicks and 9.2 % of letter flicks type a word (`mc.mjs tailP=0.15 tail=1.2`), with *d → don't*, *d → didn't*, *l → little* on top. This has to be the first thing measured, and the classifier should defend against it now (see the priority list).

### 3. At plausible thumb noise about one word stroke in twenty types a different real word

`mc.mjs` draws every word stroke in the theory, weighted by its corpus frequency, on the real key, with a per-user systematic direction bias (SD 5 degrees) plus per-stroke aim noise (SD 9 degrees), lognormal leg lengths around 4.5 mm, a second leg whose length varies (SD 0.3 in log ratio), corner angles with SD 15 degrees, rounded corners, a slight bow, sensor jitter, 120 Hz sampling and a real peak time, and maps the result through the real dictionary exactly as `Keyboard.resolve` does. With no lift-off tails, 93.1 % of word strokes type what was intended; 4.75 % type another real word (2.6 % of flicks, 8.5 % of hooks), and the rest type a letter or a mark. At 6 degrees of noise and 3 of bias the wrong-word rate is 2.0 %; at 12 and 6 it is 9.9 %. The commonest substitutions are *I → it's*, *to → your*, *I → in*, *is → it's*, *we → what*, *to → this*, *don't → going*, *the → think*. A negative contraction loses its negation 8.3 % of the time. The main driver for hooks is unequal legs, not aim: with every other source switched off, varying only the second leg's length drops hooks from 98 % to 95 %.

The document reports no real-word error rate at all, and the evaluation assumes every misread is noticed and corrected. A wrong word that reads grammatically is the error class proofreading misses, so some of these will be sent.

### 4. "Send" sits between the two page-switch flicks on the corner key

The corner key's map reads `123 · send · abc` across its top (`shots/desk-dark-rest-UT.png`). With `classify` on its 94 × 70 rect, the up-right flick to the letter page becomes "send" at 22.5 degrees of aiming error toward vertical, and so does the up-left flick to numbers. Live, after typing "I have", an up-right flick drawn 25 degrees steep sent "I have" as a message (`live_out.txt`). At 9 degrees of noise that is about 0.6 % of page switches, at 12 degrees about 3 %, and page switches are frequent because digits, `:` and `)` live on other pages. The "return to start calls it off" rule protects against aborts, not against misdirection. In the terminal context the same slip executes a half-typed command. On the space bar in the terminal, Tab (up-right) and history Up (up) are 17 degrees apart from the diagonal's centre, so a slightly steep Tab replaces the line with the previous command.

### 5. The cost model prices hooks for a different stroke, and the sensitivity only runs one way

The 702 ms hook is the research report's figure for a key-to-key glide, one acquisition plus Cao and Zhai line times for a 10 mm and a 14 mm segment (`02-hci-text-entry.md` §6.1). The hooks that were built are drawn on one key with legs of about 4.5 mm, and the 300 ms flick implies a movement of only 38 ms. Priced consistently with the same CLC constants (68.8 · L^0.469 ms), a 4.5 mm flick is about 400 ms and a 4.5 mm hook about 560 ms. The document's three variants only ever make hooks dearer (922 ms, more recall, more errors), so the reader never sees the case where hooks are cheap.

I re-ran the evaluation under several sets of assumptions (`eval2.mjs`, same corpus, planner and stages; gains are over the letter keyboard, with the baseline also paying a page switch for digits). On everyday dialogue the 48 flicks plus endings gain between 12 % and 29 % in every scenario I tried, and on real SMS between 5 % and 16 %. The 188 hooks add anything from +23 points (CLC-consistent pricing, no recall or errors) through +16 points (CLC-consistent with the "cautious" recall and errors) and +6 points (published "cautious") to about zero (922 ms hooks with the error rates from my Monte Carlo: flicks 3.6 %, hooks 12.6 %, endings 5 %) and −4 points (round one's harsh assumptions). In other words the value of the hook layer is undetermined by a factor that spans its whole contribution, and the document should say so in those terms rather than only from the pessimistic side. The "cautious" variant also assumes 4 % hook errors, below the robustness tool's own 5 to 7 % at 4.5 mm and 9 degrees, and `src/model.js` still attributes its rates to a "sloppiness 1" that no longer exists. Finally, the Numbers panel opens on everyday dialogue, a textbook corpus, with the bare-model column first (`shots/panel-numbers.png`); for a keyboard whose job is real texting, SMS and the robust range should come first.

### 6. Over-turned curls and narrow out-and-backs type a capital letter

The circle detector in `classify` runs before `classifyStroke` looks for a corner. A curl turned 125 to 135 degrees, or an out-and-back whose return is 45 degrees off, is accepted as a circle and types the key's capital (`adv.mjs`: T-n turned 130 degrees types "T"; reproduced live). The robustness output shows the same effect as "cw → circle" up to 4.3 % with a bowed path, but the document never mentions it.

### 7. Endings accept anything, and two of them are 17 degrees from each other

`Engine.ending` applies an ending to whatever word precedes the caret, so *and* plus `-ing` is "Anding" and *you* plus `-ing` is "Youing" (live). The `-ing` sector (up-right) runs from 25 to 62 degrees of elevation, so a 45 degree flick has 17 degrees of margin to `-s` and 20 to the caret band; a flick at 65 degrees turned *make* into "Makes" instead of "making" (live). The robustness tool's 93.6 % ending accuracy at 4.5 mm and 9 degrees means roughly one ending in sixteen comes out as a different valid word form, and it does not test the down-flick (finish) at all, because it filters `s` out of the directions.

### 8. The letter mode is not contained in steno mode

`DESIGN.md` says steno mode keeps every letter where it is and that the letter keyboard is contained. For lowercase letters that is true. But the return swipe that capitalises a flick letter on the letter page now types a word on most flick letters: d out and back typed "Do", u out and back typed "Our" (live). Letter flicks also now carry hook words, so the tails of finding 2 affect someone who only ever types letters. The 48 directions that used to hold symbols now hold words, so existing Wurstfinger users' symbol habits will type words. And the grid itself changed from four columns of 57.75 pt squares to three columns of 94 × 70 pt keys with a corner key, which is a new keyboard in motor terms even on the letter page. These are defensible choices, but the claim of containment should be withdrawn and the losses listed.

### 9. Common words and real texting staples have no stroke

Thirty of the 150 commonest words are spelled, 8 % of all tokens: *of, be, on, go, one, got, up, oh, or, as, did, am, an, okay, by, ok, us, who, been, over, give, down, doing, great* and others. The centre key's letters have no free directions, and the document acknowledges *of, on, one, got, did* as the "least even corner", which undersells it. In the held-out real SMS, *haha* (0.83 % of tokens), *ok* (0.70 %), *lol* (0.63 %) and *ur* (0.40 %) are among the forty commonest tokens and none has a stroke, because the geometric-mean blend deliberately suppresses what is common only in texts. For a keyboard whose purpose is "real texting", that blend throws away exactly the vocabulary of texting; an SMS-weighted layer or a texting pack that includes these as strokes would fix it. Three free turn sets remain unused (A-se back, all of I-sw, all of E-e).

### 10. The live preview flashes the wrong word on sloppy hooks

With clean strokes the preview is stable, but with the designer's 4.5 mm and 9 degree thumb, 20.7 % of hooks show some other word on the way before the hook is recognised: *aren't* flashes *at*, *I'll* flashes *in* (`flicker.mjs`). That is the diagonal fallback of finding 1 appearing during the second leg. A preview that shows the wrong word undermines the reassurance it exists to give.

### 11. The confusability term does not model confusion

`alike` in `tools/theory.mjs` penalises two flick words on neighbouring directions only when they share at least half of a prefix, scaled by the smaller benefit. It does not use the probability of a misread between the two directions, ignores grammatical substitutability (*he/him/her*, *this/to/your*, *is/it's/I'm* all remain neighbours), ignores negation, and ignores the hook-to-diagonal confusion that dominates finding 3. The clarity metric the research report asked for is still not reported.

### 12. Smaller defects and unsupported statements

- On SMS, letters-only steno mode is 2 % slower than the letter keyboard, which the document attributes to digits and marks being "a page or a curl away". Digits are a page away on the current keyboard too, but the baseline charges them nothing; charging both sides the same page switches halves the loss to 1 %.
- A top-row chip is clipped by the strip (`shots/desk-dark-I-nw-cw-50.png`), and curl chips are often pushed several rows away from the lit cell by the overlap stepping (`shots/mobile-light-A-sw-ccw-80.png`), which weakens the "each curl sits where the finger would go" property.
- The curl timer starts when a direction is first established, not when the finger pauses, so "pausing for a quarter of a second shows the curls" describes it inaccurately; a slow first leg also triggers it.
- The round-trip test's skip rate (33 %, 60 % of SMS) should be stated beside the "3,034 messages" claim, and SMS casing is rewritten before testing.
- The robustness test uses one key, weights all directions equally rather than by use, never tests the live space-bar latch, the finish stroke, or the corner key, and still halves `peakTime`.
- "The four outcomes … each own a ninety-degree sector of turn angle, which is scale-free" holds for the angle, but the length gates (`minFirst` 14 pt, `minSecond` 12 pt) are absolute, and the circle detector takes part of the hook sector.

## Novelty

I checked again for word-level gestures in MessagEase and Thumb-Key and found none; MessagEase is discontinued and Thumb-Key's slide gestures are editing commands. Touch steno for phones exists only as chorded multi-touch boards (Plover's touchscreen stenotype plugin, Steno Keyboard Pocket), which is the approach the design rightly rejects. The closest prior art remains KeyScretch (Costagliola, Fuccella and colleagues, IUI 2011, IEEE THMS 2014), in which each gesture starts on a soft-keyboard key, is guided by that key's radial menu, enters a frequent multi-letter chunk, and has compound strokes for longer chunks; and Isokoski's menu-augmented soft keyboards (CHI 2004), which put marking menus on keys. T-Cube and MessagEase supply tap plus flick on nine targets, marking menus and OctoPocus the hesitation guide, AAC morphology keys the inflection layer, and Plover the translator.

What is new, as far as I can establish, is the combination: a corpus-optimised, deterministic word layer living in the symbol directions of a MessagEase letter grid, so that the word mode is a superset of the letter mode for lowercase spelling; endings as meanings on the word-boundary key that replace the space rather than add a stroke, with the same left-is-not and right-is-is convention as the curls; and a Plover-style translator with per-context dictionary stacks under a thumb keyboard. That is a well-argued design increment. It is not a new interaction primitive, and a programme committee would expect the contribution to rest on a measured motor and learning study, which is still where the real novelty would be.

## Learnability

The first hour will go well, for the same reasons as before and one new one. The 48 flick words are printed on the keys in a second colour, the gesture is the one the user already makes for flick letters, and the words are the ones they type dozens of times an hour, so I expect *the, you, to, I'm, it's, and, that* to be adopted within minutes. The hesitation guide is now consistent with the marking-menu literature: it appears on a rest, shows what each direction does, and following it is the expert movement. That is good design and I would keep it unchanged.

The first week is where it will stall, and nothing in this iteration addresses that. A hook word is used a median 4.6 times a week at 1,000 words a day, so the six to ten exposures that the literature finds before first recall take about two weeks per hook. Seventy percent of hook use has no relation to its flick beyond a first letter, which is the arbitrary-code case the research report's own rule 9 warns against. There is no record of which strokes a person knows, no schedule that introduces a few strokes at a time in order of personal benefit, no recall check with the guide off, and no visible progress; the only push toward strokes is a chip that shows the shorter way once after a word was spelled. Rehearsal through a guide does not by itself produce the switch from the novice method to the expert one (Gutwin, Cockburn and Lafreniere), so I expect most users to plateau at the flicks and a handful of high-frequency hooks. The vocabulary is also inconsistent in a way that matters for learning: the interface calls the gesture a "curl", and a curl drawn as a curve types a different word (finding 1). Either the recogniser should accept arcs, by classifying total turning rather than a single corner, or the interface should teach a corner.

## Design-level objections that no bug fix would cure

What is inherent to a prototype and needs measurement:

1. **The hook layer's worth is unknown.** Whether 188 curls on a 10.9 mm key are worth a learner's effort depends on their time and error rate on a thumb, which spans the whole of their modelled contribution. No further modelling will settle it.
2. **Real-word substitution is the price of a word layer without a language model.** Determinism is a virtue for trust and for a shell, but it means every motor slip on a word stroke is a valid word. The size of that cost is an empirical question, and the answer decides whether this can be the default.

What the designer could change now:

3. **The recovery story.** Make the fallback match the claim (a failed curl resolves to its own first-leg flick), widen "change" to step through the neighbouring directions' words as well, or show the two likeliest alternatives in the strip after any word stroke and let one tap replace the word.
4. **Tail tolerance.** Ignore the last few samples before lift-off when the finger decelerates, require a second leg of at least about 3.5 mm and half the first, and do not give hooks to letter flicks unless the tail defence is shown to work.
5. **Send placement.** Send must not be one aiming error from a frequent page switch.
6. **Confusability as a cost.** Replace string similarity with misread probability times how plausible and how damaging the substitution is (grammatical class, negation), and report the clarity metric.
7. **Texting vocabulary.** The blend should not suppress what people text. Give *of, on, be, go, got, up, okay* strokes using the unused turns or the centre key's out-and-backs, and add *haha, lol, ok*.
8. **The learning loop.** A curriculum driven by the person's own typing, staged introduction, recall checks and a visible adoption measure. This is the difference between a clever layer and one people adopt.

## What study is needed, and what I predict it would show

**A motor study first**, with 12 to 20 participants on their own phones using the demo in phone mode, one-thumb and two-thumb, eliciting every stroke class (taps, letter flicks, word flicks, all three turns, endings, finish, corner-key flicks and caret slides) both with the target shown and from memory, logging full kinematics. It should report per-class confusion matrices, the share of errors that produce a valid wrong word, time per class, the distribution of leg lengths and lift-off tails, and it should be used to calibrate the synthetic thumb. My prediction with the current recogniser: word flicks 95 to 97 % correct, hooks 85 to 92 %, with most hook errors landing on the neighbouring diagonal rather than their own flick; lift-off tails converting somewhere between 2 and 10 % of flicks into hooks; ending errors of 4 to 8 %; and a hook time of 450 to 650 ms, which would make hooks worth more than the pessimistic variant says and less than the bare model says.

**Then a longitudinal and field study**, with 12 to 16 participants over two to three weeks of short sessions followed by two weeks of logged real use, comparing the letter keyboard with steno mode, measuring entry rate on transcription and on composition, corrected and uncorrected error rates (including wrong words left in sent messages), stroke share by class, recall with the guide off at one day and one week, and workload. My prediction: participants adopt 20 to 35 of the 48 flicks and fewer than 30 hooks; on dialogue-like transcription they gain 8 to 15 % over letter mode, on their own messages 0 to 8 %; uncorrected wrong-word errors rise by 0.5 to 2 % of words; nobody approaches the modelled 60 to 70 WPM, and typical one-thumb typists move from about 25 to 35 WPM to about 28 to 38.

## Changes that would most raise my score, in priority order

1. **Fix the curl fallback and correct the claims.** Resolve a failed curl to its own first-leg flick, extend "change" to the neighbouring words (or offer the two likeliest alternatives after any word stroke), split the robustness output by direction, and rewrite `DESIGN.md` lines 189 to 191 and the Numbers panel note.
2. **Defend against lift-off tails**, and make the synthetic thumb honest: add tails, per-user direction bias, unequal legs, smooth arcs and over-turned corners; run every key, weighted by real stroke frequency; report the frequency-weighted wrong-word rate and the negation-loss rate.
3. **Move "send"** away from the page-switch flicks (and in the terminal, away from anything a slip can reach), and separate Tab from history Up.
4. **Re-price and report fairly.** One consistent movement model for flicks, hooks and endings; a symmetric sensitivity; error rates taken from the robustness tool, not assumed; a predicted uncorrected wrong-word rate; SMS and the flicks-plus-endings range first.
5. **Confusability as a cost**, with the clarity metric reported and *he/him/her*, *to/your/this*, *is/it's/I'm* separated.
6. **Endings and circles.** Refuse an ending that does not make a word, widen the `-ing`/`-s` margin, and run the corner test before the circle detector.
7. **Texting vocabulary.** Strokes for *of, on, be, go, got, up, okay, haha, lol, ok*; put *don't* and *can't* on the rule's turns; cover `'ll`, `'ve` and `'d`.
8. **Name the gesture consistently** and either accept arcs or teach corners.
9. **Build the learning loop**: per-user log, 10 to 15 new strokes per session in order of personal benefit, spaced re-exposure through the coach, recall checks with the guide off, and a visible adoption measure.
10. **Run the motor study** before another design iteration; the demo is already good enough to collect the data.

If items 1 to 9 were done well, I would score the work about 7.8 overall, with soundness and without-fault near 8. With a motor study that supported the recogniser and the cost model, I would go to about 8.3. A score of 9.2 requires the longitudinal study to show that people adopt the strokes and come out faster without sending more wrong words, because that is the claim the owner's bar is really about.
